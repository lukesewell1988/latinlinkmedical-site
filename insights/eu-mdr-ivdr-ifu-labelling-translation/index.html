Translating IFUs and labelling under EU MDR and IVDR

Under EU MDR and IVDR, the instructions for use and the labelling are part of the technical documentation. This note sets out what usually has to be translated, where language variants still matter, and how to brief a specialist medical team.

The Latin Link team · 27 November 2026 · 12 minute read

When a medical device or IVD is placed on the EU market, the instructions for use and the labelling are part of the regulatory file. They are not a brochure that can be tidied up at the end.

EU MDR (Regulation (EU) 2017/745) and IVDR (Regulation (EU) 2017/746) both require the information supplied with the device to be clearly understandable to the intended user, and they allow Member States to require that information in their official language or languages. For most manufacturers, that turns IFU translation into a controlled regulatory step: the same version discipline, terminology control and review you would expect for a clinical or quality document, not a last-mile desktop-publishing task.

This note is a practical briefing for regulatory, labelling and product teams. It is not legal advice. It sets out what usually has to be translated, where language variants still matter — including when the same product family will also go to Latin America — and how to brief a specialist medical translation team.

What actually has to be translated

The IFU is the document most teams start with, but it is rarely the only text in scope. Depending on the device and the markets, the language pack may also include:

  • Labels, sterile-barrier packaging and outer carton text
  • Warnings, contraindications, residual risks and symbols that still carry residual wording
  • Implant cards and patient information, where they apply
  • The Summary of Safety and Clinical Performance (SSCP), or the IVD equivalent, when it is made public
  • Software user interfaces, accompanying electronic IFUs, and the text that tells the user how to access an eIFU
  • Training materials or professional user guides that are supplied with the device

Electronic IFUs can be appropriate for some devices, typically those intended for professional users, but they do not remove the need for a controlled, reviewed translation. The language still has to be accurate, versioned and consistent with the paper label and the technical documentation. If a notified body or a competent authority asks to see the IFU that belongs with a given device version, “we have a file in the translation memory” is not a complete answer.

Symbols (for example those in ISO 15223-1) can reduce the amount of text on a small label. They do not replace a carefully translated IFU, and any residual wording — units, storage conditions, unique wording required by a particular market — still needs a specialist linguist and a second reviewer.

Language of the Member State is not the same as “one Spanish” or “one Portuguese”

A frequent source of delay is treating a single European language pack as if it will also serve every later market.

For the EU and the UK, teams usually need European Spanish for Spain and European Portuguese for Portugal, alongside French, Italian, German and the other official languages required for the countries of launch. Latin Link’s core medical teams work into English, Spanish (European and Latin American) and Portuguese (European and Brazilian), with strong French and Italian teams and coverage across other European and Asian languages as a project needs.

If the same device family will later be submitted in Latin America, that European pack is not automatically reusable as-is. Mexican, Argentine, Colombian or Peruvian Spanish, and Brazilian Portuguese for ANVISA, differ in terminology, units presentation, and what a regulator or a hospital user expects to read. We have written separately about the pitfalls of treating Latin American submissions as a single language problem; the same pattern appears on device labelling. Plan the EU and Latin American variants early, and keep a glossary that records which term is approved for which market.

Where IFU translations usually go wrong

Most of the issues we are asked to repair are process issues, not mysteries of grammar.

The IFU is translated in isolation. A warning on the label, a phrase in the software, and a paragraph in the IFU end up saying slightly different things. Users notice; reviewers notice sooner.

The linguist has no device or clinical context. An IFU is full of implied knowledge: the difference between a warning and a precaution, how a contraindication must be read, whether a unit or a decimal is meaningful, how a step is actually performed. Medical linguists with clinical backgrounds, who translate only within their field and only into their native language, catch those points. A generalist translator, or an unreviewed machine-translation pass, often does not.

Layout is left until last. Line breaks, heading hierarchy, table cells, trademark line spacing and “do not use if the sterile barrier is damaged” next to a symbol all change meaning when the text is flowed into InDesign or an XML-based labelling system. A linguistic review of a Word file is necessary; it is not sufficient. An in-context or print-ready check should be part of the process.

Versions drift after a design or PMCF change. A single English source IFU may go through several revisions in a year. If only some languages are updated, or if file names do not carry the same version and date, sites and distributors can be left with the wrong instructions. The discipline is the same one clinical teams already use for protocol amendments: a register of documents, versions and languages, and a review of the change in context, not just the marked-up sentence.

A process that stands up to a file review

For clinical and regulatory buyers, a defensible IFU translation looks like this:

  1. A clear brief and a frozen source. The version, the markets, paper versus eIFU, any symbol or term glossary, and the previous approved IFU if one exists.
  2. Specialist translation into the native language of the linguist, by a medical linguist with a clinical background and at least five years’ specialist experience.
  3. Independent review by a second linguist, so no one revises their own work, in a workflow aligned with ISO 17100.
  4. Terminology and memory control, so the label, the IFU, the software and any later revision stay consistent.
  5. In-context or print-ready checks, then a signed Certificate of Translation Accuracy when your technical file, notified body or distributor needs one.

That is Latin Link’s standard way of working on device projects, not an optional add-on: every IFU or labelling pack is translated by a native specialist and reviewed by a second linguist, with desktop publishing available when you need production-ready files. Same-day work is available when a revision is genuinely urgent; it still goes through translation and independent review.

How to brief a supplier (a short checklist)

When you request a quote, the work is faster and safer if the first email already includes:

  • The source IFU and labelling, with the document version and date
  • Target countries, not just language names — “Spanish for Spain and Mexico” is a brief; “Spanish” is not
  • Whether you need paper IFUs, eIFUs, software strings, or all three
  • Any approved glossary, symbol list, or previous IFU that must stay consistent
  • The delivery format (Word, InDesign, XML, screenshots) and whether a print-ready check is required
  • Whether you need a Certificate of Translation Accuracy for the technical file

You can send that pack to the Latin Link Projects Team at projects@latinlink.com, or start from our medical device translation page. If you also need the same product family prepared for ANVISA, COFEPRIS, INVIMA, ANMAT or other Latin American authorities, say so at the outset so the language variants are planned once.
