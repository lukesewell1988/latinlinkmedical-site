ISO 17100-aligned workflows and Certificates of Translation Accuracy: what clinical buyers should ask for

Sponsors, CROs and sites are often asked to show that a translated document is accurate. An ISO 17100-aligned workflow and a signed Certificate of Translation Accuracy answer that question together, but only if you know what each one covers.

The Latin Link team · 27 November 2026 · 7 minute read

Auditors, ethics committees and regulatory teams increasingly ask a simple question about translated clinical documents: how do you know it is right? Two things usually make up the answer. The first is the process the translation went through. The second is a signed statement that the finished translation is a complete and accurate rendering of the source.

This article sets out what each of those should contain, so that buyers in clinical operations, regulatory affairs and quality can ask suppliers the right questions.

What ISO 17100 actually describes

ISO 17100 is the international standard for translation services. It sets requirements for the people involved (translator and reviser competence), for the resources used, and for the steps of a translation project: preparation, translation, a translator’s own check, revision by a second linguist, and verification before release.

It is a process standard. It does not certify a single document, and it does not replace subject-matter expertise. A workflow can be aligned with ISO 17100 in that it follows those requirements in practice; that is the language Latin Link uses, and it is the language you should expect a supplier to use accurately.

The workflow you should expect for clinical content

  • Qualified, native specialists. For medical work, translators should have clinical or pharmaceutical experience and at least five years’ translation experience, and should translate only into their native language and only within their field.
  • Independent revision. A second qualified linguist compares the translation against the source for accuracy, terminology, completeness and style. This should be standard, not an optional extra.
  • Specialist review where it matters. For patient-facing materials and clinical outcome assessments, a clinical or in-country review adds a further check on usability and local practice.
  • Terminology control. An approved glossary and a translation memory for the programme, so product names, endpoints and procedures stay consistent across documents and versions.
  • Verification before delivery. Final checks on numbers, units, cross-references, formatting and completeness.

What a Certificate of Translation Accuracy is for

A Certificate of Translation Accuracy is a signed statement from the translation provider that identifies the source document and the translation, the languages, and confirms that the translation is, to the best of the provider’s knowledge, a true and complete rendering of the source. Sites, ethics committees and sponsors often file it alongside the translated informed consent form, protocol synopsis or patient materials.

A useful certificate is specific. It should name the document, its version and date, the language pair, the date of the translation, and the provider’s details. A generic certificate that does not identify the version is of limited value when an amendment arrives a month later.

What a certificate does not do

A certificate is only as good as the process behind it. It is not a substitute for independent revision, and it is not a regulatory approval. If a supplier offers certificates but cannot explain who translated and who revised the document, treat the certificate as a formality rather than evidence.

Questions to ask a supplier

  1. Who translates, and what clinical and translation experience do they have?
  2. Is every document revised by a second qualified linguist, as standard?
  3. How do you control terminology across a study and across amendments?
  4. Can you provide a signed Certificate of Translation Accuracy that identifies the document version?
  5. Do you offer back-translation and reconciliation for patient-facing materials when needed?
  6. How are files handled, stored and shared, and will you work under our NDA?
