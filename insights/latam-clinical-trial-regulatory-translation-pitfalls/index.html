Translation pitfalls in Latin American clinical trial and regulatory submissions

Most delays we see on Latin American submissions are not about the science. They come from treating Spanish or Portuguese as a single language, letting terminology drift across the trial package, or sending patient-facing text that an ethics committee cannot accept.

The Latin Link team · 26 November 2026 · 8 minute read

When a sponsor or CRO takes a study or a dossier into Latin America, the science is usually already settled. What still trips teams up is the language: a protocol amendment that no longer matches the informed consent form, a “neutral” Spanish file that a Mexican or Colombian reviewer will not accept, or a Brazilian Portuguese text that reads as if it were written for Lisbon.

Latin Link has spent 15 years translating clinical and regulatory documents for this region. The pitfalls below are the ones our team sees most often on work for COFEPRIS in Mexico, ANVISA in Brazil, INVIMA in Colombia, and neighbouring authorities such as DIGEMID in Peru and ANMAT in Argentina.

1. One “Latin American Spanish” for every authority

Spanish is not interchangeable across the region. Mexico, Colombia, Argentina and Peru share a language, but not the same regulatory vocabulary, patient tone or preferred terminology for clinical procedures, adverse events and labelling. A file prepared in a generic “LATAM Spanish” often has to be reworked when it reaches a local ethics committee or the authority itself.

The safer approach is to decide the target country before translation starts, brief the linguists on that market, and keep a country-specific glossary for the study. Where the same protocol will be used in several Spanish-speaking countries, plan for localisation of the patient-facing set, not a single compromise version.

2. Treating Brazilian Portuguese as a variant of European Portuguese

ANVISA submissions and Brazilian ethics reviews expect Brazilian Portuguese, including the terms used in Brazilian clinical practice and in ANVISA’s own resolutions. European Portuguese is a different target language. Recasting a Portugal-focused translation for Brazil, or mixing the two, is one of the fastest ways to invite questions and delay a filing.

Use native Brazilian specialists for Brazil, and native European Portuguese specialists when Portugal or Lusophone Africa is the market. The two should not share a translation memory blindly without a review for regional usage.

3. Terminology that drifts across the trial package

A protocol, investigator’s brochure, informed consent form, case report form and patient diary are one study, not five separate jobs. If each file is translated in isolation, product names, procedure names, visit schedules and endpoint wording start to diverge. Reviewers notice; so do sites and patients.

A sound process leads with translation and then an independent review, using the same specialist team, a shared translation memory and an approved glossary across the package. When an amendment arrives, the change should be traced through every dependent document, not only the protocol.

4. Patient-facing text that is accurate but not usable

Informed consent forms, patient information leaflets, recruitment materials and quality-of-life instruments have to be understood by the people who will sign or complete them. A linguistically correct translation can still fail an ethics committee if the reading level is too high, the tone is wrong for the country, or a concept has been rendered too literally.

For clinical outcome assessments and other instruments, sponsors often also need back-translation and cognitive debriefing. These are not decorative extras: they are how you check that patients in Mexico, Brazil or Colombia actually understand the same question the source instrument intended.

5. Amendments, version control and last-minute source changes

Clinical programmes move. A “final” English protocol is revised, a consent form is updated after an ethics comment, or a safety text changes late in the process. If the translated set is not versioned with the same discipline as the source, sites can be left with mixed versions, and the submission file no longer holds together.

Agree a version-naming convention at the start, keep a clear record of what has been translated and reviewed, and allow time for the second linguist to review the changed passages, not only the first translation of the delta.

6. Machine translation without a specialist in the loop

Machine translation can help with large, repetitive files and tight timelines, but raw machine output is not a regulatory document. Used without a native specialist and a second review, it tends to flatten country-specific language, mistranslate units, abbreviations and headings, and miss inconsistencies that a reviewer of the source would expect to see resolved.

Where machine translation post-editing is appropriate, every line still needs to be checked and edited by a specialist translator, then reviewed again, in a workflow aligned with ISO 17100. The standard is a process, not a stamp to put on a file.

What a sound process looks like

Latin Link is a team of linguists and scientists. Our medical linguists have clinical or pharmaceutical work experience and a minimum of five years’ translation experience. They work only into their native language and only within their field. Every project is translated by a native specialist and reviewed by a second linguist, with translation memories and glossaries held in tools such as Trados and memoQ so terminology stays consistent across the study.

That is the work we do on clinical trial documents and on regulatory submissions for Latin American authorities. If you are preparing a COFEPRIS, ANVISA or INVIMA filing, or a multi-country patient-facing set, send us the files and the target countries. We will tell you plainly what the translation and review should cover.
